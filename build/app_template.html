This ‘Intervention Cost Converter’ (v__VERSION__, last update: ) is an open access web-based tool for converting intervention costs across currencies and price years: it adjusts estimates of cost expressed in one currency and price year to a specific target currency and price year. It suits costs from any sector, including health care, public health, education, social welfare and international development, because it uses economy-wide price indices and purchasing power parities. It applies the three methods of Turner et al. (2019), Value in Health 22(9):1026–1032; the default, Method 2 with PPP and the GDP deflator, is the computation of the CCEMG–EPPI Cost Converter.

Before using the tool, please read the ‘Information for users’, located beneath the tool on this web page, or the How to use page.

To use the tool, please enter and select your data using Steps 1–6, below. The further options keep their defaults unless you change them.

Recalculate

The cost as the study reports it. In local currency: the money of the country where the cost was incurred; most studies report this. In US$ or in I$: the study already converted a local cost. The calculator first turns it back into local currency at the price-year rate, then adjusts it (Turner et al., Figure 4); applying local inflation straight to a US$ figure would overstate it.

Separators are fine (1,000,000); see Decimal mark under the further options for 2.500,50. Unit costs and totals both work: the adjustment is a multiplication. Results update as you type; Recalculate refreshes them.

All money values here are in the country's current currency unit for every year (for example euros for Germany in 1995, new leones for Sierra Leone in 2017). If the study reports an older unit (Deutsche Mark, old leones), choose it: the amount is first divided by the legal conversion rate. For dollarised economies (Ecuador, El Salvador) and economies whose accounts are kept in US$ (Liberia, West Bank and Gaza), the local currency is converted at each year's official exchange rate. The table of conversions is data/currency_history.csv in the repository.

World Bank: PPP conversion factors from the International Comparison Program (2021 benchmark), about 200 economies from 1990. A new benchmark revises every year, so a result can change when the World Bank updates the series; the calculator notes when the conversion spans a benchmark year.

OECD: PPPs for GDP for 65 mostly high-income countries, some from the 1950s. The two differ slightly for OECD members, as the IMF and OECD options do in the CCEMG–EPPI tool.

The setting where the resources were used and paid for. Its currency is the starting point and its inflation drives Method 2.

Borrowing a cost from another country's study? Choose that study's country here, and your own setting as the target currency.

International $ converts with PPP factors, so I$1 buys in each country what US$1 buys in the United States. Better for comparing resource use across countries, and the usual choice in reviews.

US$ converts at market exchange rates: widely understood, but rates can swing.

Local currency of a country: your study setting's money (as in the CCEMG–EPPI tool), or the same country to adjust for inflation only. Turner et al. suggest reporting local currency next to US$ or I$.

PPP reflects what money buys locally; the CCEMG–EPPI Cost Converter uses PPP. Market rates reflect what money buys in trade; Turner et al. (Figure 3) use them to bring costs from another setting into the study's currency.

The year whose prices the cost is expressed in: the price year the study states, or else the year the costs were collected. It is usually not the publication year. For a fiscal year such as 2015/16, use the list mode, which can average the two calendar years. Years after the data are projected.

How to read a price year written as 2015/16, 2015-16 or FY2016-style ranges. Average converts the cost at both calendar years and averages the results; First year or Second year use one of them. A single year (2015) is always read as that calendar year.

The year whose prices you want the result in, usually your analysis year. It can be earlier than the price year: the calculator then deflates. You can choose up to 2037; years after the last World Bank figure (2024 or 2025 for most countries) use projected values, shown in red.

Further options

GDP deflator, linked series (default): the price change of everything produced in the economy, with the jumps caused by rebasing the national accounts removed. WHO-CHOICE (2003) recommends the GDP deflator, and the CCEMG–EPPI tool uses it.

GDP deflator, standard series: the World Bank's headline series; jumps at rebasing years are flagged.

Consumer price index: the price change of a household shopping basket. It may not track healthcare prices.

Which index? Medical-care price indices can rise faster or slower than the economy-wide indices; where a national health-sector deflator exists, it can be typed under Values used (Dunn et al., 2018).

Official exchange rate (PA.NUS.FCRF): the annual average rate set or reported by the national authority.

Alternative conversion factor (PA.NUS.ATLS): the World Bank's own rate, which replaces the official rate in years with multiple or controlled exchange rates (for example Lebanon after 2019, Venezuela, Argentina before 2016). It also covers years after an official series stops. A warning appears where the two differ by more than 10%.

The method reported as the final result; the other two are shown as sensitivity analyses, and the methods wording follows this choice.

Method 2 (default) with PPP and the GDP deflator is the CCEMG–EPPI computation and needs no tradable share. Choose Method 1 when resources are mostly tradable or local inflation is above about 15–20% a year, and Method 3 when you know the tradable share.

20%

The part of the cost spent on tradable resources bought on world markets: imported drugs, equipment, laboratory supplies. The rest is nontradable: staff time, buildings, local services.

Method 3 adjusts the tradable part with Method 1 and the rest with Method 2. An ingredients-based costing usually shows which items were imported; Turner et al. suggest treating imported items as tradable.

Automatic reads 1,000,000 and 2,500.50 as written in English, and 12,5 or 1.234,56 as a decimal comma. A number such as 1,000 or 1.000 could be read either way; the calculator says how it read it. Choose Comma if the study writes 2.500,50, or Point to read every comma as a thousands separator. Currency signs (£, €, US$) and spaces are ignored.

IMF forecasts (default): after a country's last year of World Bank data, its price index follows the IMF World Economic Outlook inflation forecast (average consumer prices) year by year; after the last forecast year the last rate is held. Countries without a forecast use their 10-year average. 5-, 10- or 20-year average: the index grows at the country's own average annual rate over its last 5, 10 or 20 years of data.

Exchange rates: Follow inflation gap lets a currency lose value against the US$ at the gap between its projected inflation and US inflation (relative purchasing power parity); Hold last rate keeps the last observed rate. PPP factors always follow the GDP-deflator gap.

Projected results are rounded to 3 significant figures and shown with the range from the 5- and 20-year rules. A series that stopped more than 3 years before the latest data is not projected.

Information for users

This web-based tool adjusts estimates of costs from their original currency and price year to a target currency and/or price year. The price indices (GDP deflator or consumer price index) and purchasing power parities for GDP cover the whole economy, so the tool can be used for intervention costs in any sector, not only health care. Where a sector-specific price index exists (for example a health-sector deflator), it can be typed under Values used. A step-by-step guide with worked examples is on the How to use page.

How the tool works…

The tool computes each cost in up to three ways, following Turner et al. (2019). With C the cost in country S's currency at year-t0 prices and V the result in currency T at target-year t1 prices:

Method 1 V = C ÷ rS(t0) × rT(t0) × PT(t1)/PT(t0) Method 2 V = C × PS(t1)/PS(t0) ÷ rS(t1) × rT(t1) Method 3 V = s × V1 + (1 − s) × V2

r: local currency per US$ (market) or per I$ (PPP). P: GDP deflator or CPI. s: tradable share. For US$ and I$, rT = 1 and PT is the US index.

Method 1 converts first, at the price-year rate, then inflates with US prices (or the target country's prices). It suits tradable resources such as imported drugs and equipment.

Method 2 is a two-stage computation, as in the CCEMG–EPPI Cost Converter. Stage 1 adjusts the original estimate of cost from the original price year to the target price year, using the original country's price index (the implied inflation factor, IIF). Stage 2 converts the price-year adjusted cost from the original currency to the target currency, using PPP values (or exchange rates) for the target year (the implied conversion factor, ICF). With PPP and the GDP deflator this is the default and the final result.

Method 3 treats a tradable share with Method 1 and the rest with Method 2. Turner et al. recommend it when the cost can be split.

Which to choose. With local inflation above about 15–20% a year, Kumaranayake (2000) advises working in US$ (Method 1). For high-income settings, Turner et al. advise reporting in the local currency with local inflation.

Years after the data

Price and target years run to 2037. After a country's last year of World Bank data, its price index follows the IMF World Economic Outlook inflation forecasts (or its own 5-, 10- or 20-year average), exchange rates follow the inflation gap against the US, and PPP factors follow the GDP-deflator gap. Projected values are displayed in red type; results that use them are rounded to 3 significant figures, and can be recomputed with your own rates.

Data and currency units

Indices, exchange rates and PPP factors come from the World Bank, the OECD and the IMF (listed below). Every money value is in the country's current currency unit for every year: exchange rates published in an older unit (pre-euro, Bulgaria, Venezuela, Zimbabwe) are re-expressed, and an automatic check confirms that exchange rates and PPP factors agree with GDP in local currency and US$. Deflator base years differ by country but only within-country ratios are used. Statistical agencies revise past values, so results can differ slightly from published studies that used older releases.

Excel workings. Every calculation on this page is repeated as Excel formulas, with the data, so anyone can check a result: Cost_Converter_Workings.xlsx.

    How to use the Intervention Cost Converter

    From a single cost to a full list, with the worked examples of Turner et al. (2019). Every field in the calculator also has an i button that explains it in place.

    1. What the calculator does

    Costs from different years and countries cannot be compared directly: prices rise, and currencies change in value. The calculator restates a cost in the prices of one base year and in one currency, in the three ways set out by Turner et al. (2019), and shows every rate and index it uses so you can check and report the result.

    The defaults suit a systematic review: international dollars, the linked GDP deflator, and Method 2 as the main result (the CCEMG–EPPI approach), with Methods 1 and 3 as sensitivity analyses.

    2. Quick start

    1. Step 1: type the cost estimate and say whether it is in local currency, US$ or I$ (usually local currency). If the study used a former currency (DEM, old leones), choose it under Amount is in.
    2. Step 2: keep the World Bank PPP values, or choose the OECD ones.
    3. Steps 3 and 4: choose the currency (country) of the original study and the target currency.
    4. Steps 5 and 6: choose the original price year and the target price year.
    5. Keep the further options (the linked GDP deflator and Method 2) unless you have a reason to change them.
    6. Read the final result and the sensitivity analyses in the Results table, check the warnings and Values used, then copy the wording or a link to the calculation.

    3. Choosing the output currency

    • International $ (PPP): compares what money buys in each country and moves less; the usual choice for reviews.
    • US$: familiar and used in international transactions, but market exchange rates can move sharply.
    • Local currency: suits a decision-maker whose budget is in that currency. For a cost from a high-income country used in the same country, adjusting with local inflation in local currency is all that is needed. Report it next to US$ or I$; The same cost in other currencies gives all of them at once.

    4. Choosing a method

    MethodWhat it doesUse it whenWatch out for
    Method 1Converts at the price-year rate, then applies US (or output-country) inflationMost resources are tradable, or local inflation is above about 15–20% a yearUnderstates local staff and service costs when local inflation runs above US inflation
    Method 2Applies local inflation, then converts at the base-year rateMost resources are nontradable: salaries, buildings, local services. The CCEMG–EPPI default with PPPOverstates imported items when local inflation runs above US inflation
    Method 3Tradable share through Method 1, the rest through Method 2An ingredients-based costing shows which items were importedNeeds a split that published studies often do not report

    If you cannot split the cost, Turner et al. suggest Method 1 when tradable resources dominate and Method 2 when nontradable resources dominate. The gap between the methods grows with the gap between local and US inflation. Choose the method reported as the final result under the further options; the other two are labelled sensitivity analyses.

    7. Example: a cost from another country

    You cost a programme in India and borrow a UK unit cost of £1,200 at 2015 prices. Choose United Kingdom, 2015, then Local currency of a country: India, target price year 2024. Turner et al. (Figure 3) convert at market rates; the CCEMG–EPPI tool uses PPP. Both are offered next to the target country (converted with).

    8. Converting a list of costs

    1. Choose A list of costs at the top of the form.
    2. In Excel, put one cost per row: Study, Amount, Country, Price year, Currency. Copy the cells with the header row and paste them in.
    3. Check the matches under Columns; change any that are wrong.
    4. Currency: blank or the local code (INR) for local currency; USD or I$ for converted amounts; an old code such as DEM or SLL for amounts in a former currency.
    5. Price years such as 2015/16 are read as set under Fiscal years (average of the two years by default).
    6. Set the PPP source, target currency and target price year once; it applies to every row. The Notes column gives the warnings for each row.
    7. Copy table for Excel or Download CSV. The export keeps full precision, adds the rounded main result, and ends with the tool version, data vintage, series codes and settings.

    9. Typing your own values

    Use Values used when a study reports its own deflator or exchange rate, when you want a health-sector price index, or to reproduce a published adjustment. Type over the number; the box turns violet and is marked Your value.

    • Take both years of an index from the same source; bases differ between sources.
    • Clear a box to return to the data; Use the data values again clears them all.
    • Values you type also apply to A list of costs, and rows that use them say so.
    • Copy link saves the inputs, settings and your values in the page address.

    10. Price years after the data (to 2037)

    World Bank data end in 2024 or 2025 for most countries. For later years the calculator projects:

    • Price indices with the IMF World Economic Outlook inflation forecasts (to 2031, then the last rate held), or at each country's average rate over its last 5, 10 or 20 years.
    • Exchange rates following the inflation gap against the US, or held at the last rate.
    • PPP factors following the GDP-deflator gap against the US.

    Projected values are shown in red type and results are rounded to 3 significant figures, with the range from the 5- and 20-year rules. Projection assumptions lists each country's rates; type a rate to replace them. A series that stopped more than 3 years before the latest data is not projected. Projections are assumptions, not data: say so in your report.

    11. Warnings and what to do

    • Amount is more than 10% of GDP: the amount is probably in an old currency unit or in thousands. Choose the old unit under Amount is in.
    • Amount read in the current unit: the price year is before a currency change; choose the old unit if the study used it.
    • Official rate differs from the alternative factor: multiple or controlled exchange rates. Consider Alternative conversion factor.
    • Deflator rebasing break: switch to the linked GDP deflator.
    • No data, or series ends early: choose another year or source, or type a value.
    • High inflation (above 15% a year): consider Method 1 unless most of the cost is nontradable.
    • Price level outside 5–400% of the US or a PPP-to-exchange-rate ratio change above three-fold: check the values used.
    • Projected values: the result depends on the projection assumptions; report the range.

    12. Reporting

    Turner et al. ask authors to state the measure of inflation, the output currency, the adjustment method, and how costs from other settings were handled. Give the data sources and vintage, the tool version, and report the local currency too. Methods-section wording writes this for the main method, and Copy link lets readers reopen the calculation.

    13. Questions

    • Why does my result differ from the CCEMG–EPPI tool? Same formula (Method 2, GDP deflator, PPP), different data: IMF October 2023 there, World Bank, OECD and IMF 2026 here.
    • Why does it differ from a published paper? Agencies revise their series, and PPPs change with each ICP benchmark. Type the paper's values to reproduce it.
    • Can the base year be earlier than the price year? Yes; the cost is deflated.
    • What is an international dollar? A unit that buys in each country what US$1 buys in the United States.
    • Does the deflator's base year matter? No; only within-country ratios are used.
    • Can I check the calculations? Yes. Download the Excel workings: the same inputs and data, with every step as a formula you can trace (Formulas › Trace Precedents).
    • How reliable is the data? The data-quality report lists every unit conversion, deflator break, rate gap and stale series found when the data were built.
    • How is the data updated? Run python build/fetch_data.py then python build/build.py; the build stops if a unit check fails.
