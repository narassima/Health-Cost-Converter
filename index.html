The cost

The cost as the study reports it, in the currency chosen under Reported in. Commas are fine (1,000,000). Unit costs and totals both work: the adjustment is a multiplication, so it scales with the amount.

Local currency: the money of the country where the cost was incurred. Most studies report this.

US$ or I$: the study already converted a local cost. The calculator first turns it back into local currency at the price-year rate, then adjusts it (Turner et al., Figure 4). Applying local inflation straight to a US$ figure would overstate it.

The setting where the resources were used and paid for. Its currency is the starting point and its inflation drives Method 2.

Borrowing a cost from another country's study? Choose that study's country here, and your own setting under Output currency.

The year whose prices the cost is expressed in: the price year the study states, or else the year the costs were collected. It is usually not the publication year. Years after the data are projected.

Shown for euro-area countries before they adopted the euro. All money values here are in euros for every year, so an amount in the old currency is first divided by the irrevocable conversion rate fixed by the EU Council. Leave it unticked if the study already reports euros.

The costs

Copy the cells from Excel and paste them here; the columns arrive separated by tabs. A first row with column names is recognised (Study, Amount, Country, Year, Currency), so the columns can be in any order.

Country can be a name (Viet Nam or Vietnam) or an ISO code (VNM, VN). Currency: blank or the local code (VND) for local currency; USD or I$ for amounts the study already converted; an old code such as DEM for pre-euro amounts. The settings under Convert to apply to every row.

Convert to

The year whose prices you want the result in, usually your analysis year. It can be earlier than the price year: the calculator then deflates. You can choose up to 2037; years after the last World Bank figure (2024 or 2025 for most countries) use projected values, marked in green.

GDP deflator: the price change of everything produced in the economy. WHO-CHOICE (2003) recommends it, and the CCEMG–EPPI tool uses it.

Consumer price index: the price change of a household shopping basket. The next-best option, but it may not track healthcare prices. Both come from the World Bank.

US$ converts at market exchange rates: widely understood, but rates can swing.

International $ converts with PPP factors, so I$1 buys in each country what US$1 buys in the United States. Better for comparing resource use across countries, and steadier.

Local currency of a country: your study setting's money, or the same country to adjust for inflation only. Turner et al. suggest reporting local currency next to US$ or I$.

The country whose currency you want, usually your study setting. Choose the same country as above to adjust for inflation only.

Market rates reflect what money buys in trade; Turner et al. (Figure 3) use them to bring costs from another setting into the study's currency.

PPP reflects what money buys locally. The CCEMG–EPPI Cost Converter uses PPP; choose it, with the GDP deflator and Method 2, to follow that tool.

World Bank: PPP conversion factors from the International Comparison Program, about 200 countries from 1990.

OECD: PPPs for GDP for 65 mostly high-income countries, some from the 1950s. The two differ slightly for OECD members, as the IMF and OECD options do in the CCEMG–EPPI tool.

20%

The part of the cost spent on tradable resources bought on world markets: imported drugs, equipment, laboratory supplies. The rest is nontradable: staff time, buildings, local services.

Method 3 adjusts the tradable part with Method 1 and the rest with Method 2. An ingredients-based costing usually shows which items were imported; Turner et al. suggest treating imported items as tradable.

Years after the data

After a country's last year of data, its price index grows at its own average annual rate over its last 5, 10 or 20 years of data. To use a forecast instead (IMF World Economic Outlook, a central-bank target), type the rate for each country under Projection assumptions.

Follow inflation gap: a currency loses value against the US$ at the gap between its projected inflation and US inflation (relative purchasing power parity). Hold last rate: the last observed rate is kept. PPP factors always follow the GDP-deflator gap, as the World Bank does between survey years.

How to use

Step by step

Each row follows one method from the reported amount to the result, like Figures 1 and 2 of Turner et al. Boxes are amounts; arrows are the rate or inflation factor applied. Values marked projected lie beyond the data.

Values used

Every rate and index behind the result. Type over a value to use your own, for example a figure from the IMF World Economic Outlook or from the study itself; clear the box to go back to the data. Take both years of an index from the same source, because index bases differ between sources.

Projection assumptions

Annual growth used for each price index after its last year of data. Type a rate to replace the trend, for example an IMF forecast or an inflation target; clear the box to return to the trend.

Converted costs

How the calculator works

The three methods

M1 V = C ÷ rS(t0) × rT(t0) × PT(t1)/PT(t0) M2 V = C × PS(t1)/PS(t0) ÷ rS(t1) × rT(t1) M3 V = s × V1 + (1 − s) × V2

C: cost in country S's currency at year-t0 prices. V: result in currency T at base-year t1 prices. r: local currency per US$ (market) or per I$ (PPP). P: GDP deflator or CPI. s: tradable share. For US$ and I$, rT = 1 and PT is the US index.

Which method to use

  • Method 1 converts first, then inflates with US prices: suits tradable resources (imported drugs, equipment).
  • Method 2 inflates with local prices, then converts at the base-year rate: suits nontradable resources (staff, local services).
  • Method 3 treats each part with the method that suits it; Turner et al. recommend it when the cost can be split.
  • With local inflation above about 15–20% a year, Kumaranayake (2000) advises working in US$ (Method 1).

Years after the data

Price and base years run to 2037. After a country's last year of World Bank data, its price index grows at its average annual rate over the preceding 10 years (or 5 or 20), exchange rates follow the inflation gap against the US, and PPP factors follow the GDP-deflator gap. Projected values are marked in green and can be replaced with your own rates.

Data

Indices, exchange rates and PPP factors come from the World Bank and the OECD (listed below). Euro-area countries are in euros for every year; deflator base years differ by country but only within-country ratios are used.

    How to use the Health Cost Converter

    From a single cost to a full list, with the worked examples of Turner et al. (2019). Every field in the calculator also has an i button that explains it in place.

    1. What the calculator does

    Costs from different years and countries cannot be compared directly: prices rise, and currencies change in value. The calculator restates a cost in the prices of one base year and in one currency, in the three ways set out by Turner et al. (2019), and shows every rate and index it uses so you can check and report the result.

    2. Quick start

    1. Type the Amount and say what it is Reported in (usually local currency).
    2. Choose the Country where incurred and the Price year.
    3. Under Convert to, choose the Base year and Output currency.
    4. Keep the GDP deflator unless you have a reason to use CPI.
    5. Set the Tradable share for Method 3.
    6. Read the three results, check Values used, then copy the wording.

    3. Choosing the output currency

    • US$: familiar and used in international transactions, but market exchange rates can move sharply.
    • International $ (PPP): compares what money buys in each country and moves less, but is less familiar and estimated between survey years.
    • Local currency: suits a decision-maker whose budget is in that currency. Report it next to US$ or I$; The same cost in other currencies gives all of them at once.

    4. Choosing a method

    MethodWhat it doesUse it whenWatch out for
    Method 1Converts at the price-year rate, then applies US (or output-country) inflationMost resources are tradable, or local inflation is above about 15–20% a yearUnderstates local staff and service costs when local inflation runs above US inflation
    Method 2Applies local inflation, then converts at the base-year rateMost resources are nontradable: salaries, buildings, local servicesOverstates imported items when local inflation runs above US inflation
    Method 3Tradable share through Method 1, the rest through Method 2An ingredients-based costing shows which items were importedNeeds a split that published studies often do not report

    If you cannot split the cost, Turner et al. suggest Method 1 when tradable resources dominate and Method 2 when nontradable resources dominate. The gap between the methods grows with the gap between local and US inflation.

    5. Example: the paper's figures

    VND 1,000,000 incurred in Vietnam in 2006, wanted in US$ at 2016 prices, 20% tradable.

    US$ 73.05 · 107.59 · 100.68

    The button enters the paper's rates and indices under Values used. With today's World Bank data Method 2 gives US$ 132.50, because Vietnam's GDP deflator has since been revised (now × 2.91 from 2006 to 2016, against 2.36).

    6. Example: a cost reported in US$

    A study reports US$ 100 for Vietnam in 2006; you want 2016 US$. Choose Reported in: US$. The calculator converts back to VND 1,599,425 at the 2006 rate, applies Vietnamese inflation for Method 2, and converts at the 2016 rate (Figure 4 of the paper).

    US$ 116.84 · 172.09 · 161.04

    7. Example: a cost from another country

    You cost a programme in India and borrow a UK unit cost of £1,200 at 2015 prices. Choose United Kingdom, 2015, then Local currency of a country: India, base year 2024. Turner et al. (Figure 3) convert at market rates; the CCEMG–EPPI tool uses PPP. Both are offered under Convert with.

    8. Converting a list of costs

    1. Choose A list of costs at the top of the form.
    2. In Excel, put one cost per row: Study, Amount, Country, Price year, Currency. Copy the cells with the header row and paste them in.
    3. Currency: blank or the local code (INR) for local currency; USD or I$ for converted amounts; an old code such as DEM for pre-euro amounts.
    4. Set Convert to once; it applies to every row. The Notes column says when a row failed or used projected values.
    5. Copy table for Excel and paste into your extraction sheet. In the downloaded file, Download CSV also works.

    9. Typing your own values

    Use Values used when a study reports its own deflator or exchange rate, when you want IMF World Economic Outlook figures, or to reproduce a published adjustment. Type over the number; the box turns violet and is marked Your value.

    • Take both years of an index from the same source; bases differ between sources.
    • Clear a box to return to the data; Use the data values again clears them all.
    • Values you type also apply to A list of costs.

    10. Base years after the data (to 2037)

    World Bank data end in 2024 or 2025 for most countries. For later years the calculator projects:

    • Price indices at each country's average annual rate over its last 10 years of data (or 5 or 20).
    • Exchange rates following the inflation gap against the US, or held at the last rate.
    • PPP factors following the GDP-deflator gap against the US.

    Projected values are green. Projection assumptions lists each country's rate; type a forecast to replace it (the IMF World Economic Outlook projects to 2030). Projections are assumptions, not data: say so in your report and test other rates.

    11. Warnings and what to do

    • No data for a country in a year: choose another year, switch PPP source to World Bank, or type a value.
    • High inflation (above 15% a year): consider Method 1 unless most of the cost is nontradable.
    • Price level outside 5–400% of the US: the PPP and exchange-rate series are in different currency units that year (a redenomination, as in Zimbabwe, Venezuela or Liberia). Check or type values.
    • PPP-to-exchange-rate ratio changed more than three-fold: usually a redenomination, large devaluation or very high inflation.
    • Amount read as euros: tick the pre-euro box if the study reports the old currency.
    • Projected values: the result depends on the projection assumptions.

    12. Reporting

    Turner et al. ask authors to state the measure of inflation, the output currency, the adjustment method, and how costs from other settings were handled. Give the data sources and retrieval date, and report the local currency too. Methods-section wording writes this for the method you choose.

    13. Questions

    • Why does my result differ from the CCEMG–EPPI tool? Same formula (Method 2, GDP deflator, PPP), different data: IMF October 2023 there, World Bank and OECD 2026 here.
    • Why does it differ from a published paper? Agencies revise their series. Type the paper's values to reproduce it.
    • Can the base year be earlier than the price year? Yes; the cost is deflated.
    • What is an international dollar? A unit that buys in each country what US$1 buys in the United States.
    • Does the deflator's base year matter? No; only within-country ratios are used.
    • Can I check the calculations? Yes. Download the Excel workings: the same inputs and data, with every step as a formula you can trace (Formulas › Trace Precedents).
    • How is the data updated? Re-export the five series with Dataset Finder and run _build/build.py.
